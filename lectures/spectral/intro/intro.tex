\chapter{Spectral methods: Galerkin and collocation}

Spectral and finite-element methods both approximate a solution in a finite-dimensional space. Spectral bases usually extend over the whole domain, while standard finite-element bases have local support. Spectral-element methods combine high-degree approximation with a subdivision into elements.

The convergence rate depends on regularity and on the basis. With the appropriate periodic or endpoint compatibility, $C^\infty$ smoothness gives convergence faster than every fixed algebraic power. Analyticity in a neighbourhood of the domain gives geometric (exponential) convergence; some entire functions converge still faster. Smoothness alone does not imply an exponential rate. We develop these distinctions using Fourier and polynomial approximation; see \cite{CanutoHussainiQuarteroniZang2011}.

For our spectral methods we will use either Fourier bases or polynomial bases.
Within this choice of bases we will use two techniques
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Galerkin methods.

          We preserve the functional formulation of the problem (e.g., a variational equation like \eqref{eq:Galerkin elliptic variational}), but we reduce the functional space.

    \item Collocation methods

          \begin{quote}
              The idea is to choose a finite-dimensional space of candidate solutions (usually polynomials up to a certain degree) and a number of points in the domain (called collocation points), and to select that solution which satisfies the given equation at the collocation points.

              \url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collocation_method}
          \end{quote}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}
    The name \emph{spectral} makes reference to the fact that these bases are eigenfunctions (i.e., related to the \emph{spectrum}) of some differential operator: Fourier functions are eigenfunctions of the Laplacian $-\frac{\diff^2}{\diff x^2}$ and the polynomial bases we select are eigenfunctions of \emph{Sturm--Liouville} operators. But we will use them to deal with general operators.
\end{remark}

\section{Collocation methods in a practical example}

\begin{example}[Collocation for the Laplace equation using Chebyshev polynomials]
    \label{ex:collocation Laplace Chebyshev}
    Consider the Laplace equation
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{dcases}
            -\frac{\diff^2 u}{\diff x^2}(x) = f(x) & \text{for all } x \in (-1,1) \\
            u(-1) = u(1) = 0.
        \end{dcases}
    \end{equation*}
    Let us look for an approximate polynomial solution $u_N \in \mathbb R_N[x]$.
    To recover the $N+1$ coefficients uniquely we need $N+1$ independent equations.

    The collocation method consists in selecting $N+1$ points $x_0=1>x_1>\cdots>x_N=-1$ and selecting the $u_N$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{dcases}
            -\frac{\diff^2 u_N}{\diff x^2} (x_j) = f(x_j)
             & \text{for all } j \in \{1, \dots, N-1\} \\
            u_N(-1) = u_N(1) = 0.
        \end{dcases}
    \end{equation*}
    For any choice of $x_k$ we can look for $u_N$ expanded as a combination of monomials
    \begin{equation*}
        u_N (x) = \sum_{k=0}^N c_k x^k.
    \end{equation*}

    Depending on the choice of $x_k$, it might be better to pick a different basis.
    If we select as collocation points
    $x_j=\cos(j\pi/N)$,
    (the \emph{Chebyshev--Gauss--Lobatto} points in \Cref{ex:Chebyshev Gauss-Lobatto}),
    then it is more suitable to use the basis of Chebyshev polynomials (see \Cref{sec:Chebyshev polynomials})
    \begin{equation*}
        u_N(x) = \sum_{k=0}^N a_k T_k(x).
    \end{equation*}
    Notice that, by construction, $T_k (x_j) = \cos \frac{kj \pi}{N}$, so the algebraic system is easier to write and solve.
    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{convergenceChebyshev}
        \caption{Maximum error sampled on an independent uniform grid of 20,001 points for the sine example in the accompanying notebook. The rapid decay eventually reaches floating-point accuracy. This sampled maximum approximates the $L^\infty$ error; it is not the exact norm.}
    \end{figure}
\end{example}
For the heat equation we can follow the same reasoning to get a spatial semi-discretisation.

\section{Linear algebra representation of spectral collocation methods}
\label{sec:spectral collocation linear algebra}

Given a basis $\phi_k$, $k \in Z \subset \mathbb Z$, such that a large enough class of ``reasonable'' functions $v \in V$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation*}
    v(x) = \sum_{k \in Z} \hat v_k \phi_k(x),
\end{equation*}
we take a finite set $Z_N = \{k_0, \dots, k_N\} \subset Z$ and we look for solutions
\begin{equation*}
    u_N(x) = \sum_{k \in Z_N} \hat u_k \phi_k(x).
\end{equation*}
Let $V_N$ be the space of such functions.

\bigskip

Galerkin's method usually leads to equations for the coefficients $\hat u_k$. However, collocation methods on the points $x_0, \dots, x_N$ are written in terms of the values $u_N(x_j)$.
Let us write this in matrix form as
\begin{equation*}
    u_N(x)
    = (\phi_{k_0} (x), \dots, \phi_{k_N}(x))
    \vec {\hat u}_N,
    \qquad \text{where }\vec{\hat u}_N
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \hat u_{k_0} \\ \vdots \\ \hat u_{k_N}
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
and so we can write a system of linear relations between $\hat u_{k}$ and $u_N (x_i)$
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        u_N(x_0) \\ \vdots \\ u_N(x_N)
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \underbrace{
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \phi_{k_0} (x_0) & \cdots & \phi_{k_N}(x_0) \\
                             & \vdots                   \\
            \phi_{k_0} (x_N) & \cdots & \phi_{k_N}(x_N) \\
        \end{pmatrix}
    }_{\mat A_N}
    \vec{\hat u}_N,
\end{equation*}

In the spectral bases we will use we have that $\frac{\diff \phi_k}{\diff x} \in V_N$ if $k \in Z_N$. Hence we can write, for some matrix $\mat B_N$,
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{\diff \phi_{k_0}}{\diff x} (x)
        \\
        \vdots \\
        \frac{\diff \phi_{k_N}}{\diff x} (x)
    \end{pmatrix}
    = \mat B_N^t
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \phi_{k_0} (x) \\ \vdots \\ \phi_{k_N}(x)
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
and hence
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\diff u_N}{\diff x} (x)
    =  \left(\frac{\diff \phi_{k_0}}{\diff x} (x), \dots, \frac{\diff \phi_{k_N}}{\diff x} (x)\right)
    \vec{\hat u}_N
    = (\phi_{k_0} (x), \dots, \phi_{k_N}(x)) \mat B_N \vec {\hat u}_N.
\end{equation*}
Then we have that
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{\diff u_N}{\diff x}(x_0) \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\diff u_N}{\diff x} (x_N)
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \underbrace{\mat A_N \mat B_N \mat A_N^{-1}}_{\mat D_N}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        u_N(x_0) \\ \vdots \\ u_N(x_N)
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
We can iterate this process to deduce that
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{\diff^2 u_N}{\diff x^2}(x_0) \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\diff^2 u_N}{\diff x^2} (x_N)
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \mat D_N^2
    \begin{pmatrix}
        u_N(x_0) \\ \vdots \\ u_N(x_N)
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}


If the basis $\phi_k$ is orthogonal in some scalar product, the elements of $\mat B_N$ are easy to compute.


\begin{remark}
    \label{rem:pointwise evaluation plotting}
    Pointwise evaluations are suitable for plotting. However, if we wanted to recover the basis coefficients we can use $\mat A_N^{-1}$.
\end{remark}

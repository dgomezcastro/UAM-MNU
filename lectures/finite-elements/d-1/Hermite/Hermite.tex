\section{Hermite cubics}
\label{sec:Hermite cubics}

In order to have a $C^1$ global interpolator we need to select a different basis.

\paragraph{Hermite cubics in $(0,1)$}
Let us take $K = (0,1)$.
We take $\mathcal P = \mathbb R_3[x]$.
We pick as nodal functions
\begin{equation*}
    N_1 (v) = v(0), \qquad N_2(v) = v(1), \qquad N_3(v) = \frac{dv}{dx} (0), \qquad N_4(v) = \frac{dv}{dx} (1).
\end{equation*}
Our nodal basis will be $\theta_0, \cdots, \theta_3$ such that
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{matrix}
        \theta_0(0) = 1, & \theta_0(1) = 0, & \theta_0'(0) = 0, & \theta_0'(1) = 0
        \\
        \theta_1(0) = 0, & \theta_1(1) = 1, & \theta_1'(0) = 0, & \theta_1'(1) = 0
        \\
        \theta_2(0) = 0, & \theta_2(1) = 0, & \theta_2'(0) =1,  & \theta_2'(1) = 0
        \\
        \theta_3(0) = 0, & \theta_3(1) = 0, & \theta_3'(0) =0,  & \theta_3'(1) = 1
    \end{matrix}
\end{equation*}
Solving for the exact values leads to the functions in \Cref{fig:Hermite cubic nodal basis}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.5\textwidth]{Hermite_reference_element.png}
    \caption{Nodal basis for the Hermite cubics}
    \label{fig:Hermite cubic nodal basis}
\end{figure}

The local interpolator is given by
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal I_{K} v (x) = v(0) \theta_0(x) + v(1) \theta_1(x) + \frac{dv}{dx} (0) \theta_2 (x) + \frac{dv}{dx} (1) \theta_3 (x).
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Hermite cubics on $(a,b)$}
Due to \Cref{rem:FEM affine equivalence element with derivatives} we will not apply \Cref{thm:FEM affine equivalence and interpolation estimate}, so we perform the estimate in a generic interval.

Let us take $K = (a,b)$.
We take $\mathcal P = \mathbb R_3[x]$.
We pick as nodal functions
\begin{equation*}
    N_1 (v) = v(a), \qquad N_2(v) = v(b), \qquad N_3(v) = \frac{dv}{dx} (a), \qquad N_4(v) = \frac{dv}{dx} (b).
\end{equation*}
As discussed in \Cref{ex:FEM affine equivalence Hermite cubics}, this is not affine equivalent to the element in $(0,1)$. However, the shape functions are
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        \psi_1(x) & = \theta_0 (\tfrac{x-a}{b-a}),       & \qquad \psi_2(x) & = \theta_1 (\tfrac{x-a}{b-a}),       \\
        \psi_3(x) & = (b-a) \theta_2 (\tfrac{x-a}{b-a}), & \qquad \psi_4(x) & = (b-a) \theta_3 (\tfrac{x-a}{b-a}).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Local interpolation in an interval $K$}
In dimension $d=1$ we have that $\rho_K = h_K$.
We can use \Cref{thm:FEM bound interpolation error} to deduce that
\begin{equation*}
    [v - \mathcal I_K v]_{W^{j,p} (K)} \le C h_K^{4-j}[v]_{W^{4,p}(K)}, \qquad j \in \{0, \cdots, 4\}.
\end{equation*}


\paragraph{$H^2$-global interpolation}
Since our local interpolator preserves the value of the function and its derivative at the end points of each interval, we can build a non-trivial basis of $\mathcal P_{\mathcal T} \cap H^2(a,b)$.
Hence, given a domain $\Omega = (a,b)$ and sub-division $\mathcal T$ made of intervals, the global interpolator will yield
$$
    \mathcal I_{\mathcal T} : C^1( [a,b] ) \to C^1([a,b]), \qquad \mathcal I_{\mathcal T} : H^2(a,b) \to H^2(a,b).
$$
Let us take a sub-division of $(a,b)$ of the form $a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_{N+1} = b$. Let us describe the finite-element space generated by using Hermite cubics in each interval to generate $C^1$ functions. To this end, we build a basis made out of $v_i$ and $w_i$.
We have to properly ``glue'' by setting
$$
    v_{i}(x_j) = \delta_{ij}, \qquad v'_i (x_j) = 0, \qquad w_i (x_j) = 0, \qquad w_i'(x_j) = \delta_{ij}
$$
This leads to the basis elements $\varphi_{2i} = v_i$ and $\varphi_{2i + 1} = w_i$ with
$$
    v_{i} (x) =
    \begin{cases}
        \theta_1 \left( \frac{x-x_{i-1}}{x_{i}-x_{i-1}} \right) , & x \in [x_{i-1},x_{i}) \\
        \theta_0 (\frac{x-x_{i}}{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}) ,                & x \in [x_{i},x_{i+1}) \\
        0,                                                        & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
    \qquad
    w_{i} (x) =
    \begin{cases}
        (x_{i}-x_{i-1}) \theta_3 \left( \frac{x-x_{i-1}}{x_{i}-x_{i-1}} \right) , & x \in [x_{i-1},x_{i}) \\
        (x_{i+1}-x_{i}) \theta_2 (\frac{x-x_{i}}{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}) ,                & x \in [x_{i},x_{i+1}) \\
        0,                                                                        & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
$$
See \Cref{fig:Hermite cubics basis functions}.
Then we have the interpolant
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal I_{\mathcal T} u(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{N+1} \Big( u(x_i) v_i(x) + \frac{du}{dx}(x_i) w_i (x)\Big).
\end{equation*}
Notice that $\mathcal I_{\mathcal T} u(x_j) = u(x_j)$ and $\frac{d \mathcal I_{\mathcal T} u}{dx} (x_j) = \frac{du}{dx}(x_j)$ for all points in the mesh.

\begin{remark}
    If we only want to build a basis of $\mathcal P_{\mathcal T} \cap H^1(a,b)$ we would take the basis of $\mathcal P_{\mathcal T} \cap H^2(a,b)$ and add, at each interior node $x_i$, an element $z_i$ given by only one of the two pieces of $w_i$ (e.g. $(x_{i+1} - x_i) \theta_2 (\frac{x-x_{i}}{x_{i+1}-x_{i}})$ on $[x_i, x_{i+1})$ and $0$ elsewhere), which has a discontinuous derivative at $x_i$.
    This is a highly unnatural and unpleasant task that we will not undertake.

    This is not a good idea since the point of moving to this basis is approximating $u$ that is very regular.
    If $u$ is not $C^1$ then we cannot use $\mathcal I_{\mathcal T}$. If $u$ is $C^1$ regular then $\mathcal I_{\mathcal T} u \in \mathcal P_{\mathcal T} \cap C^1([a,b])$.
    And we want $u$ to be more than $C^1$ regular in order to get any rates.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{$H^2$-global-interpolation error}
Notice that due to the $C^1$-glueing, the interpolant is at most $H^2$.
We have for $j \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ that
\begin{align*}
    [v - \mathcal I_{\mathcal T} v]_{H^j (a,b)}
     & = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{N+1} [v - \mathcal I_{\mathcal T} v]_{H^j (x_{i-1},x_i)}^2 \right)^{\frac 1 2}
    \\
     & \le \left( C \sum_{i=1}^{N+1} (x_{i} - x_{i-1})^{2(4-j)} [v]_{H^4 (x_{i-1},x_{i})}^2 \right)^{\frac 1 2} \\
     & \le C h_{\mathcal T}^{4-j}[v]_{H^4 (a,b)}.
\end{align*}
Using Céa's lemma as in \Cref{ex:Galerkin Laplace piecewise linear Cea}, the $H^1$ estimate yields a convergence rate $h_{\mathcal T}^3$ for the Galerkin approximation.

\paragraph*{Nodal interpretation}
There are two types of nodal functions
\[
    N_{i}^{(0)} (v) = v(x_i), \qquad N_{i}^{(1)} (v) = \frac{\diff v}{\diff x} (x_i).
\]

\paragraph*{Numerical implementation passing to the reference element}
Let us consider $\Omega = (a,b)$ and $a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_{N+1} = b$, and the notation $\varphi_{2i} = v_i$ and $\varphi_{2i + 1} = w_i$ for the basis functions. To compute the mass matrix we need
\[
    m_{ij} = \int_\Omega \varphi_{i} \varphi_{j} = \sum_{k=0}^N \int_{x_k}^{x_{k+1}} \varphi_{i} \varphi_{j}.
\]
Each interval can be projected back to the reference element.
Let us write $h_k = x_{k+1} - x_k$ and use the change of variables
\begin{equation*}
    x = x_k + h_k \xi, \qquad \xi \in (0,1), \qquad \diff x = h_k \diff \xi.
\end{equation*}
On $K_k = (x_k, x_{k+1})$ only four basis functions do not vanish, namely $v_k, v_{k+1}, w_k, w_{k+1}$, and
\begin{equation*}
    v_k (x) = \theta_0 (\xi), \qquad v_{k+1} (x) = \theta_1 (\xi), \qquad w_k (x) = h_k \theta_2 (\xi), \qquad w_{k+1} (x) = h_k \theta_3 (\xi).
\end{equation*}
In other words, the restrictions of the basis to $K_k$ are the shape functions $\psi_1, \cdots, \psi_4$ of $(a,b) = (x_k,x_{k+1})$.
It is convenient to write this as a local-to-global map: for the local index $\alpha \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ we define
\begin{equation*}
    g(k,0) = 2k, \qquad g(k,1) = 2(k+1), \qquad g(k,2) = 2k+1, \qquad g(k,3) = 2(k+1)+1,
\end{equation*}
and the scaling factors $s_{k,0} = s_{k,1} = 1$ and $s_{k,2} = s_{k,3} = h_k$, so that
\begin{equation*}
    \varphi_{g(k,\alpha)} (x) = s_{k,\alpha} \, \theta_\alpha \left( \frac{x - x_k}{h_k} \right), \qquad x \in K_k.
\end{equation*}
Since $\frac{\diff}{\diff x} = \frac{1}{h_k} \frac{\diff}{\diff \xi}$ we deduce
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{x_k}^{x_{k+1}} \frac{\diff^r \varphi_{g(k,\alpha)}}{\diff x^r} \frac{\diff^r \varphi_{g(k,\beta)}}{\diff x^r} \diff x
    = s_{k,\alpha} s_{k,\beta} h_k^{1-2r} \int_0^1 \frac{\diff^r \theta_\alpha}{\diff \xi^r} \frac{\diff^r \theta_\beta}{\diff \xi^r} \diff \xi,
    \qquad r \in \{0, 1, 2\}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, we only need to compute once and for all the reference matrices $\widehat M, \widehat A, \widehat B \in \mathbb R^{4 \times 4}$
\begin{equation*}
    \widehat M_{\alpha \beta} = \int_0^1 \theta_\alpha \theta_\beta, \qquad
    \widehat A_{\alpha \beta} = \int_0^1 \theta_\alpha' \theta_\beta', \qquad
    \widehat B_{\alpha \beta} = \int_0^1 \theta_\alpha'' \theta_\beta''.
\end{equation*}
Using the explicit formulas
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        \theta_0 (\xi) & = 1 - 3 \xi^2 + 2 \xi^3, & \qquad \theta_1 (\xi) & = 3 \xi^2 - 2 \xi^3, \\
        \theta_2 (\xi) & = \xi - 2 \xi^2 + \xi^3, & \qquad \theta_3 (\xi) & = - \xi^2 + \xi^3,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
we obtain
\begin{gather*}
    \widehat M = \frac{1}{420}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        156 & 54  & 22 & -13 \\
        54  & 156 & 13 & -22 \\
        22  & 13  & 4  & -3  \\
        -13 & -22 & -3 & 4
    \end{pmatrix},
    \\
    \widehat A = \frac{1}{30}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        36  & -36 & 3  & 3  \\
        -36 & 36  & -3 & -3 \\
        3   & -3  & 4  & -1 \\
        3   & -3  & -1 & 4
    \end{pmatrix},
    \qquad
    \widehat B =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        12  & -12 & 6  & 6  \\
        -12 & 12  & -6 & -6 \\
        6   & -6  & 4  & 2  \\
        6   & -6  & 2  & 4
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{gather*}

Let us assume that $i = 2 I$ and $j = 2 J$, i.e. $\varphi_i = v_I$ and $\varphi_j = v_J$.
Since $\supp v_I = [x_{I-1}, x_{I+1}]$, the product $v_I v_J$ vanishes unless $|I - J| \le 1$.
Then
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $J = I$, the two intervals $K_{I-1}$ and $K_I$ contribute. In $K_{I-1}$ we have $v_I = \theta_1(\xi)$ and in $K_I$ we have $v_I = \theta_0(\xi)$. Hence
          \begin{equation*}
              m_{2I, 2I} = h_{I-1} \widehat M_{11} + h_{I} \widehat M_{00} = \frac{156}{420} (h_{I-1} + h_I),
          \end{equation*}
          where at the end points $I = 0$ and $I = N+1$ we only keep the term corresponding to the interval inside $(a,b)$.
    \item If $J = I + 1$, only $K_I$ contributes, where $v_I = \theta_0(\xi)$ and $v_{I+1} = \theta_1(\xi)$. Hence
          \begin{equation*}
              m_{2I, 2(I+1)} = h_I \widehat M_{01} = \frac{54}{420} h_I.
          \end{equation*}
    \item If $|I - J| \ge 2$ then $m_{2I, 2J} = 0$.
\end{itemize}
Similarly, if $i = 2I$ and $j = 2J + 1$, i.e. $\varphi_i = v_I$ and $\varphi_j = w_J$, we have
\begin{align*}
    m_{2I, 2I+1}     & = h_{I-1}^2 \widehat M_{13} + h_I^2 \widehat M_{02} = \frac{22}{420} (h_I^2 - h_{I-1}^2), \\
    m_{2I, 2(I+1)+1} & = h_I^2 \widehat M_{03} = -\frac{13}{420} h_I^2,                                          \\
    m_{2I, 2(I-1)+1} & = h_{I-1}^2 \widehat M_{12} = \frac{13}{420} h_{I-1}^2,
\end{align*}
and if $i = 2I + 1$ and $j = 2J+1$, i.e. $\varphi_i = w_I$ and $\varphi_j = w_J$, we have
\begin{align*}
    m_{2I+1, 2I+1}     & = h_{I-1}^3 \widehat M_{33} + h_I^3 \widehat M_{22} = \frac{4}{420} (h_{I-1}^3 + h_I^3), \\
    m_{2I+1, 2(I+1)+1} & = h_I^3 \widehat M_{23} = -\frac{3}{420} h_I^3.
\end{align*}
The remaining entries follow by symmetry $m_{ij} = m_{ji}$.
Notice that the entries have different powers of $h$ depending on whether they involve values or derivatives. This is a consequence of the fact that $w_i$ is of size $h$.

\paragraph*{Assembly}
In practice we do not compute the entries one by one as above. Instead, we loop over the elements and add the local contributions.
The global mass matrix $M \in \mathbb R^{2(N+2) \times 2(N+2)}$ is computed as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Set $M = 0$.
    \item For $k = 0, \cdots, N$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ do
          \begin{equation*}
              M_{g(k,\alpha), g(k,\beta)} \gets M_{g(k,\alpha), g(k,\beta)} + h_k \, s_{k,\alpha} s_{k,\beta} \widehat M_{\alpha \beta}.
          \end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}
The same loop with $h_k^{-1} \widehat A$ (resp. $h_k^{-3} \widehat B$) instead of $h_k \widehat M$ yields the stiffness matrix $a_{ij} = \int_\Omega \varphi_i' \varphi_j'$ (resp. the matrix $b_{ij} = \int_\Omega \varphi_i'' \varphi_j''$, which appears in fourth-order problems posed in $H^2$).
For the right-hand side $f_i = \int_\Omega f \varphi_i$ we loop in the same way and add
\begin{equation*}
    f_{g(k,\alpha)} \gets f_{g(k,\alpha)} + h_k \, s_{k,\alpha} \int_0^1 f(x_k + h_k \xi) \theta_\alpha (\xi) \diff \xi.
\end{equation*}
The resulting matrices are banded: the entry $(i,j)$ is non-zero only if $\varphi_i$ and $\varphi_j$ share an interval, so each row has at most $6$ non-zero entries.

Dirichlet boundary conditions $u(a) = u(b) = 0$ only affect the degrees of freedom of $v_0$ and $v_{N+1}$, so we remove the rows and columns $0$ and $2(N+1)$. The degrees of freedom of $w_0$ and $w_{N+1}$ remain free.
If we also impose $u'(a) = u'(b) = 0$ (as for a clamped beam) we also remove the rows and columns $1$ and $2(N+1)+1$.

\begin{remark}[Gaussian quadrature]
    The integrands $\theta_\alpha \theta_\beta$ are polynomials of degree $6$.
    Hence, if we want to compute the integrals numerically (for example, when there are variable coefficients), we need a quadrature that is exact at least for polynomials of degree $6$.
    Due to \Cref{thm:Gauss integration}, Gaussian quadrature with $4$ points is exact for polynomials of degree $7$, so it is the natural choice.
    For $\widehat A$ and $\widehat B$ the integrands have degree $4$ and $2$, so $3$ and $2$ points suffice, respectively.
\end{remark}

\section{B-splines}
\label{sec:FEM Bsplines d=1}

The idea behind B-splines is that the basis elements $\varphi_i$ are splines: piece-wise polynomials of degree $p$ in each $(x_{k}, x_{k+1})$, with $C^{p-1}$-glueing, and the smallest support possible.

\subsubsection{The B-spline basis} Let $t_0 < t_{1} < \cdots < t_m$ be a sequence of points (the \emph{knots}).
The B-splines of degree $p$ are defined by the Cox--de Boor recursion formula
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:B-spline}
    \begin{align}
        B_{i,0} (x) & = \begin{dcases}
                            1 & \text{if } x \in [t_i, t_{i+1}) \\
                            0 & \text{otherwise},
                        \end{dcases}
        \\
        B_{i,p} (x) & = \begin{dcases}
                            \frac{x-t_i}{t_{i+p} - t_i} B_{i,p-1}(x) + \frac{t_{i+p+1}-x}{t_{i+p+1} - t_{i+1}} B_{i+1,p-1}(x)
                              & \text{if } x \in [t_i, t_{i+p+1}) \\
                            0 & \text{otherwise}.
                        \end{dcases}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
With this construction we have that $B_{i,p} \in C^{p-1} (\mathbb R)$ and $\supp B_{i,p} = [t_i, t_{i+p+1}]$.
Their key property is that
\begin{equation*}
    \sum_{i=0}^{m-p-1} B_{i,p}(x) = 1
\end{equation*}
for all $x \in [t_p,t_{m-p}]$.
We build approximations of the form
\begin{equation*}
    S_p (x) = \sum_j c_{j,p} B_{j,p}(x).
\end{equation*}
For degree $p > 1$ we do not have a nice closed formula for the interpolation and typical approximations rely on least-squares optimisation.

\subsubsection{Linear B-splines}
If we take a mesh $x_0, \dots, x_N$ and knots $t_i = x_i$, then $B_{i-1,1}$ coincides with the hat function $\varphi_i$ of \Cref{ex:piece-wise linear in d=1}.

\subsubsection{Quadratic B-splines}

\paragraph{The finite element} Because of the structure, it is natural to pick the reference element $K = (-1,1)$.
Consider the uniformly spaced knots $t_i = 2i - 5$ for $i = 0, \dots, 5$. Exactly three quadratic B-splines, $B_{0,2}, B_{1,2}, B_{2,2}$, do not vanish on $K$, and their restrictions to $K$ are the shape functions
\begin{align*}
    \psi_1(y) & = \frac 1 8 ( 1- 2y + y^2)   \\
    \psi_2(y) & = \frac 1 4 ( 3 - y^2 )      \\
    \psi_3(y) & = \frac 1 8 ( 1 + 2y + y^2).
\end{align*}

We will not discuss the nodal functions $\mathcal N$.

\paragraph{Finite-elements space }
Since we have $C^1$ glueing, given a sub-division $\mathcal T$ of an interval $(a,b)$,
by the finite-elements space of quadratic B-splines people usually refer to $\mathcal P_{\mathcal T} \cap C^1([a,b])$.

\paragraph{Basis of the finite-elements space on a uniform mesh}
Another approach to constructing B-splines on uniform meshes is to define $b_0(x)$ as the characteristic function of $[0,1)$ and
\begin{equation*}
    b_p(x) = \int_{x-1}^x b_{p-1}(s) \diff s.
\end{equation*}
The support of $b_p$ is $[0,p+1]$.
Then, the B-splines of degree $p$ on the grid $x_i = i h$ are precisely $b_{p,i,h}(x) = b_p (\frac{x-x_i}{h})$, and for a sub-division $\mathcal T$ of $(a,b)$ by such a grid
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal P_{\mathcal T}\cap C^{p-1} ([a,b]) = \left\{ \sum_{i\in \mathbb Z} c_i b_{p,i,h} \Big|_{(a,b)} : c_i \in \mathbb R \right\}.
\end{equation*}
Notice that, due to the compact support, only finitely many terms are non-zero in $(a,b)$, so this space is finite-dimensional.

\paragraph{Finite-elements space}
If we take the sub-division $\mathcal T$ corresponding to $x_0 = a, \dots, x_N = b$,
the basis is obtained precisely by adding enough fictitious points outside $(a,b)$, see \Cref{fig:Bspline quadratic basis}.

\subsubsection{Cubic B-splines}
We can follow a similar procedure to obtain the cubic B-splines given, for example, in \Cref{fig:Bspline cubic basis}.
The shape functions on $K = (-1,1)$ are recovered from the cubic B-splines with uniformly spaced knots
$t_i = 2i - 7$ for $i = 0, \dots, 7$: exactly four of them, $B_{0,3}, \dots, B_{3,3}$, do not vanish on $K$, and their restrictions
$\psi_1, \dots, \psi_4$ are given by
\begin{align*}
    \psi_1 & = \frac 1 {48} (-x^3 + 3x^2 - 3x + 1)   \\
    \psi_2 & = \frac 1 {48} (3x^3 - 3x^2 - 15x + 23) \\
    \psi_3 & = \frac 1 {48} (-3x^3 -3x^2 + 15x + 23) \\
    \psi_4 & = \frac 1 {48} (x^3 + 3x^2 + 3x+1).
\end{align*}
Again, we will not discuss the nodal functions in $\mathcal N$.
